## Core thesis (where the three agree)

- The best late-stage private companies are compounding faster than any
  public comparable, and the IPO is a liquidity event, not the value
  creation event. Value accrues to whoever owned the company through the
  private-to-public transition.
- The AI cycle is unique because competitors at every layer of the stack
  exist as public and private companies at once. You cannot underwrite a
  private name without the public comps, and you cannot judge the public
  names without seeing the private pipeline. This is the argument for
  crossover investing.
- Frontier matters. Gerstner and Baker both hold that the large majority
  of model-layer revenue accrues at the frontier, and frontier leadership
  is unstable and can flip inside one product cycle.
- Energy and physical inputs are the binding constraint. Gerstner ties the
  ultimate AI total addressable market to power. Baker holds that the
  wafer bottleneck at TSMC is what prevents a bubble-style overbuild.

## Gerstner's underwriting questions

- Is growth accelerating because of AI workflows? If a software business
  cannot show that, it faces lower terminal value and higher disruption
  risk. He wants intelligence-correlated assets (Databricks, Snowflake,
  Anthropic) over legacy models.
- Is the revenue real? Since October 2025 he has flagged circular revenue
  and sham transactions in the AI buildout as red flags. Vendor financing,
  compute credits, and round-trip contracts get discounted.
- Does the math math? June 2026: roughly $1.5T of capex against about
  $300B of AI revenue. He argues the market underestimates demand, but he
  makes the bear case explicitly and expects the reader to as well.
- How do you size a pre-IPO position? On the SpaceX IPO he walked through
  the bull and bear case (can revenue 8x in four years), post-IPO
  drawdowns, lock-up expiries, and sizing. Treat that segment as the
  template.

## Baker's discipline

- Risk is LLCC: liquidity, leverage, concentration, crowding. Every
  private position is scored on all four, and illiquidity is priced, not
  ignored.
- Conviction-adjusted risk/reward drives sizing. Turnover should track
  volatility, not the calendar.
- Repeat player. Private markets reward multi-year relationships and
  treating founders fairly across many interactions. His stated rule: a
  six-month relationship before investing more than $5M; smaller checks
  are how the relationship is built.
- "Panic early or double down late. You have to be one of the two." Decide
  which before the position moves.
- Theses are falsifiable hypotheses. Analysts are judged on fact-gathering
  quality, because deep knowledge is what lets you act rationally in a
  drawdown.
- Watch the physical layer: TSMC capacity decisions, whether the buildout
  is funded by cash flow or debt, chip utilization, GPU lifespan, power
  availability. Value accrues to owners of scarce inputs before it
  accrues to application builders.
- Position bias caveat. Atreides is long most of what it discusses. Use
  the framework, pressure-test the conclusions.

## Wood's frame

- Start from the technology cost curve and the five-year TAM, then ask
  which private companies sit on the steepest part of the curve. ARK's
  Big Ideas 2026 names AI agents, robotaxis and autonomy, next-gen data
  centers, reusable rockets, blockchain rails, and multiomics.
- Own the platform before the IPO. ARKVX top holdings at May 31, 2026:
  SpaceX 11.4%, OpenAI 8.5%, Anthropic 6.4%, then Tenstorrent, Kalshi,
  Replit, Ayar Labs, Figure AI, Absci, Cellares.
- Harvest when asymmetry fades. ARK trims public names when a run has
  removed the asymmetry, and treats biotech as a trading arena rather than
  a forever holding.
- Know the wrapper. ARKVX charges a 2.75% management fee plus service and
  other expenses, and redeems only 5 to 25 percent of shares per quarterly
  window. Access is not the same as liquidity.

## How to run a late-stage private analysis

1. Stage and structure. Round, post-money, preference stack, pro rata,
   secondary versus primary, lock-up and expected IPO window.
2. Gerstner test. Is growth AI-accelerated, is the revenue real, does the
   capex-to-revenue math close for this company specifically?
3. Baker test. LLCC score, position in the physical stack, who owns the
   scarce input, what would falsify the thesis.
4. Wood test. Where on the cost curve, what five-year TAM, which public
   comps and which ARKVX or Altimeter positions already express the view.
5. Sizing and exit. Conviction-adjusted size, panic-early or
   double-down-late rule chosen in advance, post-IPO drawdown and lock-up
   plan.
6. Flag the mark. Late-stage marks lag public comps; say whether the last
   round price still holds against current public multiples.
